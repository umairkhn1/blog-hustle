How to Start a Blog in 2026 Without Wasting Six Months

The parts of starting a blog that actually decide whether it survives year one, in the order that matters. No fluff, no forty-item checklist.

JM
Jessica Miller Founder & Editor
October 6, 2026 11 min read

Most people who start a blog quit within five months. Not because they were lazy, but because they spent the first three of those months on tasks that do not compound: picking a theme font, tweaking a sidebar widget, reading about monetization before they had a single reader.

This guide is the version I wish someone had handed me in 2019. It is ordered by leverage. Each step earns you something that makes the next step easier.

1. Pick a topic you can write 100 posts about

The test is not "will this make money." The test is: can you name forty sub-topics off the top of your head, right now, without researching? If you cannot, you will run out of ideas around post nine, and that is when blogs die.

Write your topic as one sentence: I help [who] do [what] so they can [outcome]. "I help new graphic designers put together a portfolio that gets interviews" is a topic. "Design" is not a topic.

Pick a narrow starting point and let it widen. A blog about one niche inside a niche ranks faster than a general blog, because the competition is smaller and you sound credible sooner. My own site started as a blog about freelance writing rates before it grew into a general publishing site.

2. Get the boring foundation done in one afternoon

Domain, hosting, and platform. That is it. Everything else waits.

  • Domain: a real .com if you can get one. Short, no hyphens, no numbers. Do not spend three days on this.
  • Hosting: a host with one-click WordPress, or a static setup if you are comfortable with code. You need a working site today, not a perfect stack.
  • Platform: WordPress if you want plugins and total control, a static generator if you are technical and want speed. Both work. Choose one and stop comparing.

Install exactly one theme. Write your about page and set a clear navigation. Then move on. A plain site publishing every week beats a beautiful site publishing never.

3. Build a structure search engines can read

You do not need advanced SEO to launch, but you do need the basics installed correctly on day one, because retrofitting them is miserable.

  • One topic per page. Every article gets its own URL.
  • Descriptive URLs, not numbers. /start-a-blog/ beats /p?id=482.
  • Each page has one H1, and it matches the intent of the article.
  • Category pages that group related posts, with 5 to 10 posts per category.
  • A sitemap submitted to Google Search Console, which takes four minutes and tells you what is actually broken.

That last one is the most ignored and most useful. Search Console will show you which pages Google indexed, which it ignored, and which queries brought people in. It is the only performance report you will have for the first six months.

4. Write your first ten posts as a cluster, not a list

New blogs fail to rank because each post sits alone with no internal links and no clear topic. Instead of ten random articles, pick one subject and write ten posts about it.

For a personal finance blog, that cluster might be "how credit scores work." The posts would be: what a credit score is, how to check it free, which habits hurt it, how long negative marks stay, how to dispute an error, how to build credit with no history, why your score dropped suddenly, how to raise it in ninety days, what lenders actually look at, and a glossary of terms.

Now every post has somewhere to link, the cluster signals expertise to search engines, and a reader who lands on one page has nine reasons to stay. This is the single highest-leverage decision in the first three months.

The 90-day rule. Plan on getting zero meaningful traffic for three months and no income for six. Any plan that needs results sooner will end in quitting. Set a decision date at day 180 based on whether the process is working, not on whether you have earned a specific amount.

5. Install five tools, not twenty

  1. Google Search Console: indexing and query data.
  2. A privacy-friendly traffic analytics tool: page views, referrers, nothing more.
  3. A keyword tool with a free tier: used once or twice a week, not daily.
  4. An image compressor: speed is a ranking factor and a reader factor.
  5. An email list provider from day one, even with four subscribers. Starting the list after you need it means starting late.

Every additional tool is a place to spend time instead of writing. Most successful blogs run on five things and a calendar.

6. Pick a publishing rhythm you can hold for a year

Weekly is the standard advice, and it is right for most people, but only if the posts are genuinely useful. Two-monthly analysis beats four weekly listicles. What matters is that the schedule survives a bad week.

My rule: publish on a fixed day, keep a buffer of two finished drafts, and have a bank of ten outlines ready. The buffer is what saves the schedule when life intervenes, and life always intervenes.

7. Measure the right thing in month one

Not income. In month one, only two numbers matter:

  • Published posts. A simple count. If it is zero, nothing else can be fixed.
  • Email subscribers. Even ten is proof that a stranger wants more.

Traffic will be noise for a while. Page views in month one mostly measure how much you told friends about the site.

What to ignore for now

Ignore monetization strategies until you have about twenty posts and a hundred email subscribers. Ignore backlink outreach until you have something worth linking to. Ignore social media platforms you do not enjoy. Ignore every "guru" earning report that starts with a screenshot of revenue and never shows the traffic, the team, or the five years before it.

Start a blog because you want to own a body of work about something you understand. The income is a consequence of doing that for long enough, in one direction.
