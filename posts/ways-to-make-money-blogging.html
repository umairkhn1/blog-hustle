12 Realistic Ways to Make Money Blogging, Ranked by Effort

Display ads are the worst-paying option available to a new blog. Here are eleven better ones, ordered roughly by how soon a small site can start.

RC
Ryan Cole Monetization Writer
September 29, 2026 10 min read

There is a persistent myth that a blog earns money by showing ads. In practice, display advertising pays a small blog almost nothing: a site getting 20,000 page views a month might clear a hundred dollars if the traffic is in a cheap category. Meanwhile the same site could earn that from a single well-placed affiliate link or one client.

Here are twelve models that work for independent publishers, ordered roughly by how quickly a small site can realistically start.

1. Freelance services attached to your expertise

The fastest money is the money a reader pays you directly. If you write about resume design, offer resume reviews. If you write about wedding planning, offer planning calls. Services need no traffic threshold, no approval process, and no platform taking a cut.

The catch is that services do not scale with your time. Most publishers use services to fund the site during the first year, then reduce them as content income grows.

2. Digital products

Templates, spreadsheets, checklists, printable packs, notion systems, small guides. Anything that saves a reader an afternoon sells, often between $9 and $49. You need an audience in the low hundreds, not thousands.

The mistake is building a forty-page ebook nobody asked for. Build the thing your readers keep asking about in the comments and in your inbox.

3. Affiliate marketing

You recommend a product, a reader buys through your link, you earn a commission. Simple in principle, and it rewards the exact behavior that makes a blog useful: specific recommendations backed by real use.

Two requirements most people skip. First, only recommend tools you have used, because readers can tell. Second, pick programs with recurring commissions where possible, since a $20 monthly commission from a tool your reader keeps paying for compounds quietly.

4. Sponsored posts and newsletter placements

Once you have a defined audience and evidence of engagement, brands will pay for access. A blog with 8,000 monthly readers in a specific niche can charge more than a general blog with 40,000. Rates vary wildly, from a few hundred dollars to several thousand for a dedicated newsletter send.

Always label sponsored content. Beyond being legally required in most places, readers forgive sponsorship and never forgive deception.

5. Coaching and consulting

Higher ticket than a digital product, lower volume than a course. One or two calls a week can be a meaningful income line for a small site in a professional niche. Your blog is the proof of expertise that makes the call easy to sell.

6. Memberships and paid communities

A recurring community fee works when readers want ongoing access to other readers, not just to you. Expect the first twelve members to need personal invitations. Sub-$10 memberships also have high churn, so the useful range tends to start higher.

7. Courses

The best-performing small publisher model over a long period, and the slowest to earn from. Courses work when you have already answered the same question fifty times in public and the reader can see the outcome you produce.

8. Licencing content to brands

Some publishers earn more from rewriting their own articles for corporate blogs than from ads. If a company needs twelve pieces a month and you already own the expertise, that is a retainer, not a side gig.

9. Job boards and directories in a niche

If your audience is looking for work, a simple curated list of openings is genuinely useful and chargeable to employers. Small niche boards with 30 to 200 listings can be modest but steady businesses.

10. Selling your own physical product

Print, apparel, tools. Low margin, high operational overhead, and the fastest way to discover whether your audience buys anything. Do not start here unless the niche truly demands a physical product.

11. Data and research reports

Original surveys get cited, and citations build authority. Once you have a report, brands will pay to sponsor its distribution or be featured inside it. Slow to set up, unusually durable afterwards.

12. Selling the site itself

A blog with stable traffic, an email list, and clean revenue history is a sellable asset, often at 28 to 40 times monthly profit. This is an exit, not a strategy, but it changes how you should treat bookkeeping: keep clean records and separate business finances from the beginning.

The concentration trap. Publishers who built everything on one traffic source lost most of their income in a single month when an algorithm changed. Aim for three channels: search, email, and one social platform you actually like. If one drops, you still have a business.

How to decide which one

Answer two questions. Who is reading this? And what are they already paying for, in time or money? A blog for freelancers sells tools that save billable hours. A blog for new parents sells convenience and reassurance. Match the model to what your reader already purchases, and the rest gets much less speculative.

Pick one model. Run it for ninety days before adding a second. The publishers who struggle are rarely short on ideas. They are short on follow-through on any single one.
