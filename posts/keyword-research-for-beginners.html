Keyword Research for Beginners: Finding Topics You Can Actually Rank For

Volume is the least useful number in the tool. A practical method for finding keywords a small blog can win, using mostly free resources.

AB
Amanda Brooks SEO Lead
September 15, 2026 12 min read

Most keyword research advice starts with volume. That is the wrong number to start with, because a keyword with 40,000 monthly searches and difficulty 78 is not an opportunity, it is a wall. The useful question is narrower: which searches can a site with your current authority realistically win?

Four things a keyword needs to be worth chasing

  1. Real intent. Someone searching "best budget microphone for podcasting" wants a recommendation. Someone searching "podcast" wants a definition. Only one of those can be turned into a useful article.
  2. Enough volume to matter. For a new site, 100 to 800 monthly searches is a fine target. Ten of those a month is a real audience.
  3. Difficulty you can match. Not an abstract score, but the actual strength of the pages currently ranking.
  4. A path to income. If nothing you could sell relates to the topic, the traffic is decoration. That is fine for some pages, but do not build a whole site that way.

Finding keywords without paid tools

You can build a full content plan with free sources. Start with the search bar itself.

  • Autocomplete: type your topic and note what Google suggests. Each suggestion is a real query with real volume.
  • People Also Ask: expand every question in the box and record the follow-ups that appear. These are question-shaped and often low competition.
  • Related searches: the block at the bottom of the results page. Two minutes of clicking can produce twenty topics.
  • Reddit and niche forums: the exact phrasing people use when they are confused is usually better than tool phrasing.
  • Your own Search Console: once a site has traffic, this is the best tool available. Filter for queries where you rank between positions 8 and 20. You already have partial relevance there, so small improvements move real numbers.

Judging difficulty by hand

Open the results for your keyword and look at what actually ranks. Ignore the difficulty score in the tool, which is a model, and look at the pages.

  • If the top five results are all big brands with teams, skip it.
  • If two or three are forum threads, thin pages, or old articles that do not match the query, you have a shot.
  • If the top results are individual blogs of a similar age and size to yours, that is your sweet spot.
  • Look at the most recent ranking page. If the best result on page one is from 2018 and never updated, a thorough modern version can win.

Check the date, not the word count. Two pages with identical information rank differently all the time. The one that answers the question faster, is easier to scan, and was updated more recently tends to win. Word count is an output, not a target.

The one-keyword-one-page rule

Each article should target exactly one primary search intent. If you find two topics in your plan that essentially ask the same thing, merge them into one better article.

The most common new-blogger mistake is writing three thin posts that each cover a third of a subject. Three thin posts lose to one complete one, every time, and you cannot fix it later with internal links.

Map keywords to the funnel

Group your list into three buckets and plan the mix deliberately:

  • Problem aware: "why does my blog get no traffic." High volume, low commercial value, excellent for building an audience.
  • Solution aware: "how to research keywords for free." Medium volume, medium value, the bulk of a healthy content plan.
  • Product aware: "best keyword tool for small blogs." Low volume, high commercial value, where affiliate income lives.

A site made only of the third bucket looks like an affiliate storefront and struggles to earn trust. A site made only of the first gets traffic and no income. Aim for roughly half solution-aware, a third problem-aware, and a fifth product-aware.

When to go after bigger terms

Once a handful of small terms are ranking on page one, revisit the head terms. You will now have supporting pages, internal links pointing at the new piece, and evidence of topical coverage. That combination is what makes an ambitious article viable; publishing it first, on a site with no coverage, is usually why it fails.

Practical order of operations: pick ten related small keywords, publish ten solid posts over ten weeks, interlink them tightly, and review Search Console at the end. Whatever starts ranking tells you where to widen next.
